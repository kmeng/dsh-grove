# PKGS guide to personal knowledge and growth

[简体中文](PKGS.zh.md) · [English](PKGS.en.md) · [Grove README](../README.en.md)

## Purpose

PKGS (Personal Knowledge & Growth System) is a method for long-term personal growth. It builds on the knowledge and reasoning you retain. With AI as an optional collaborator, it connects exploration, knowledge management, research, independent judgment, creation, and reflection. Its aim is to improve how you understand, investigate, judge, and communicate. You can leave an idea open and return to it when you encounter new material.

Start with paper, plain Markdown, or a note-taking tool you already use. Grove is a lightweight implementation in DeepSeek Harness and Obsidian. The method below works at the pace and level of detail that fit your subject. [Original design discussion](https://chatgpt.com/s/t_6af7c6721c0081919564b556c5e5dcd3)

## From theory to practice

| Idea | What to do with it |
| --- | --- |
| Personal knowledge management | Explain what you read in your own words and find a situation where you can apply or test it. |
| Zettelkasten | Give a standalone idea its own note and describe how it connects to other ideas. Keep complete articles and primary material where their context matters. |
| Metacognition | Ask why you hold your current view, what it assumes, and what would change your mind. |
| Self-regulated learning | Decide what you want to understand, monitor how your approach works, and adjust after reviewing what you learned. |
| SECI model of knowledge creation | Write down what you learned through experience, discuss it, connect it to prior knowledge, and test your understanding in practice. This borrows ideas from a model of organizational knowledge creation. |
| Human–AI collaboration and cognitive offloading | Let AI organize material, compare explanations, and raise objections. Check the basis for each judgment yourself. |

Eight principles follow from these ideas. Put personal growth first. Explore across subjects and disciplines. Give ideas time to develop. Leave space to stop without producing anything. Keep evidence for important claims. Connect related knowledge. Revise your views through reflection. Retain control over your data and judgments.

## Three connected activities

- **Exploration.** Start with an observation, intuition, or uncertainty. Ask questions to identify the underlying problem, its assumptions, and plausible explanations. You can pause, resume, or stop when you have enough clarity.
- **Personal knowledge growth.** Record sources as you read, explain ideas in your own words, and connect them to earlier concepts or examples. Test your understanding through recall, use, and reflection. Connections often lead to new questions.
- **Research and creation (GRC).** When you choose to investigate further, define the question, map relevant concepts and theories, gather evidence, compare explanations, form a judgment, test the argument, prepare an account of what you learned, and review what to keep. Choose methods that suit the discipline and question, and revisit earlier stages when needed. You decide whether to publish or produce a deliverable.

These activities draw on a body of knowledge that you maintain. With authorization, AI can supply context, compare material, and surface counterevidence. Keep records in a format you can move between tools so that work can continue when you change models or software. Revisit earlier judgments, notice what changed, then return to exploration with that understanding. Each pass can use only the steps you need.

## Organizing knowledge and evidence

Use stable identifiers for eight kinds of reusable material: inspirations, questions, concepts, theories, claims, evidence, methods, and outputs. Research projects connect questions, evidence, and outputs. Reflection records capture how your understanding changed. Add dates, topics, and sources to important records, and state what relationship a link expresses and which version it points to.

| Record | Minimum useful detail |
| --- | --- |
| Question | Write what you want to understand and link to the observation and related concepts. |
| Evidence | Record the source, the precise location, and what you actually observed. Specify which version of a claim it supports, refutes, or qualifies. |
| Claim | State your judgment, key assumptions, and current status, such as awaiting verification, disputed, or revised. Keep the previous version and your reason for changing it. |

Record source material, AI summaries, and personal judgments with their origin and verification status. Even with a credible source, check whether its contents support the inference you are making. When your judgment changes, review what supported it before and which step the new evidence affects.

## Working with AI

| Role | AI can help with | You decide |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Thinking partner | Questions, associations, and alternative explanations | What to explore next |
| Knowledge assistant | Concept explanations, organization, and possible connections | What you understand and how to apply it |
| Research assistant | Search directions, evidence organization, and comparisons of methods | Whether the scope, methods, and evidence fit the question |
| Critical reviewer | Counterexamples, gaps in reasoning, and possible bias | Which objections change your view |
| Creation assistant | Structure, drafting, and revision | The claim, its expression, and whether to publish |

You initiate the work by default, and AI can suggest what to do next. When delegating, specify the scope, the materials it can access, and the expected result. You control long-term saves, changes to important judgments, deletion, and publication. See the [plugin details](DETAIL.en.md) for Grove’s actual access boundaries.

## Start without the plugin

1. **Capture an idea.** Write, “Why do decisions slow down in some teams as they grow?” Add the observation that prompted it.
2. **Explore freely.** List possible explanations, such as more participants, more approval steps, or unclear decision authority. Work through them yourself or ask AI to pose questions. Keep a question for later if you find one worth returning to.
3. **Learn and connect.** Explain coordination cost and decision authority in your own words. Give each an example and link both notes to the original question.
4. **Research if useful.** Compare teams with different approaches to delegation. Record checkable sources and precise locations, and link them to the claim. If you first attribute the slowdown to team size and later find that delegation explains some of the variation, save a new version with the counterexample and the conditions where the claim holds. This is an example of the method.
5. **Reflect and create.** When you need to share what you learned, write a short account of the question, evidence, and judgment. Later, ask what you understand better, what changed your mind, and which question you want to revisit. Use the answers to choose your next exploration.

A plain Markdown file is enough. Start with four lines for your question, current understanding, source or item to check, and next step. Add dates, evidence relationships, and reasons for revisions when you want to preserve the history of your reasoning.
